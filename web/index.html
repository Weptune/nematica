An atlas of one question, in seven parts

What makes a real brain different from an artificial one?

We put living nervous systems and artificial neural networks through the same tests, and judge both against one yardstick: how much real animals differ from each other. Seven questions follow. Each has an answer you can see.

How we ask.

The same three rules run through every question. They are what makes a brain and a network comparable at all.

The same instrument

A recording is a brain seen through an instrument. So every network is recorded the way the animal was: the same number of cells, the same noise, the same blind spots.

The same yardstick

Two real animals never match exactly. A copy passes when it sits no further from an animal than another animal would. That spread is the only unit we use.

Watch, then touch

Looking like a brain at rest is one test. Answering like one when a single cell is switched on is another. We ask for both, and they do not agree.

I The Echo, under Mercury, the messenger

Touch one neuron. Does the copy answer the way a real brain does?

How we asked

In 113 worms, single neurons were switched on with light, one at a time, while the rest of the brain was recorded. We built copies of the worm brain. Some learned only by watching its ongoing activity. Some also learned from those pokes. Then we scored each copy's answers against real worms.

What we saw

Copies that only watched pass up to 5 of 6 tests of how a brain looks, then answer a poke almost at random. Copies that also learned from pokes answer as well as a second worm would.

0.01agreement with real worms for copies that only watched
0.10for copies that learned from pokes, and for one real worm against another

II The Push, under Mars, who acts

Can watching a brain tell you which way a cell pushes it?

How we asked

For each of 39 mice we built a copy of its cortex from resting activity alone, and asked it what would happen if one kind of inhibitory cell were switched on. Then light switched those cells on. Every test was written down before the data was opened.

What we saw

At rest everything rises and falls together, so the copy expected activity to rise, as it would for almost any cell. Switching on Sst cells did the opposite in 29 of 29 mice. Switching on Vip cells, which silence other inhibitors, did raise activity, in 10 of 10. The copy could not tell the two apart.

77%of brain regions the copy expected to rise when Sst cells were switched on
13%that did

Open. Watching points the wrong way.A copy taught by light pulses recovers part of the pattern (0.57 against −0.06 for the watcher in Sst mice), but no copy has yet been shown to answer like a second mouse.

III The Face, under Venus, who holds the mirror

Is a good copy of an animal still that animal, or just an average one?

How we asked

Each of 27 freely moving worms got its own model. We then pulled every model toward the average worm, step by step, and measured two things: how well it forecasts the worm's next moment, and how individual it stays, where 1 means as distinct as real worms are from each other.

What we saw

The best forecaster is the least individual. It sits closer to every worm than worms sit to each other. Let each worm keep its own dynamics and the copy is as individual as a real worm, for a forecasting cost of under one percentage point.

0.78individuality of the copy tuned to forecast best
0.99of the copy that keeps each worm's own dynamics; real worms are 1

Closed by keeping individuals.The faithful copy passes 7 of 8 tests in a nested check. One lab's worms; not written down in advance.

IV The Long Memory, under Saturn, the slow one

Does a brain hold on to the past longer than a network does?

How we asked

In 35 mice at rest we measured slow memory: how much of the brain's activity drifts slowly, over seconds, instead of flickering from moment to moment. Then the same in an artificial network, recorded through a simulated copy of each mouse's own instrument, mouse by mouse.

What we saw

The instrument matters more than the mouse: seen through five mice's instruments, one network moved by 1.8 times the spread between mice. Compared fairly, a random network has less slow memory than the mouse in 33 of 35. Given strong slow drives it has more than every mouse measured. Cortex sits in between.

33of35mice with more slow memory than a random network recorded like them
1.8×how far the instrument alone moved one network, in units of the spread between mice

Closed by a slow drive.A drive carrying about 6% of each unit's variance brings the network to within 0.03 of the spread between mice in 18 held-out animals, though the interval is still wide.

V The Inner Light, under the Sun

Does a brain carry more of its own inner state than a network?

How we asked

While 49 mice watched the same film, we measured how much of the brain's state is its own and not locked to what it sees. Then the same for recurrent, gated, liquid, spiking, transformer and state-space networks, each recorded the way the mouse was. The claims were written down in advance.

What we saw

Artificial networks land on both sides of the brain. Some carry far less inner state than cortex, some more, and transformers of different sizes straddle the median mouse. The brain is not at the end of the scale.

0of4claims that cortex carries more, slower inner state that held up
59%of the median mouse's state is internal; networks range from 13% to 86%

Never there. Networks sit on both sides of the brain.Written down in advance: none of four claims of more, slower internal state held.

VI The Waning, under the Moon

Ask the same thing twice. Does the answer fade?

How we asked

Whenever the same worm neuron was poked twice within two minutes, we compared the second answer with the first: 56 pairs in 37 animals.

What we saw

A real worm's second answer is about half the first. An artificial network answers the same every time. A network whose input tires with use reproduces the fade, but this data cannot say whether it is the neuron that tires or the light tool.

Open. The neuron, or the tool?Every direct poke delivers light and activity together.

VII The Journey, under Jupiter, the far traveller

Does a copy of a still animal hold once the animal moves?

How we asked

The recipe that passes on held-still worms was refitted from scratch on 30 freely moving worms from another lab, and put through the same six tests of ongoing activity.

What we saw

On the worms it was built on, the recipe passes 5 or 6 of 6. On moving worms it passes 1 of 6, fewer than a plain copy fitted to rest alone. Movement, or the lab, changes what a brain's activity looks like.

Open. Still and moving worms differ.There are no pokes in the moving-worm data, so only resting activity could be tested.

The answer so far.

All seven on one sheet. Brass is the spread between real animals. A ring is a plain network. A filled mark is the same network once it was given what it lacked.

real animalsa plain artificial networkgiven what it lacked

So far, what separates these artificial networks from real brains is what they were shown and what they were built to carry. Not that they are artificial.

3differences closed
1never there
3still open

Three differences closed once the network was given what it lacked: pokes to learn from, its own individuality, a slow drive. One was never there.

Three are open, and each waits on data that is not yet public: stimulation in more animals, in animals that move, and with tools that can tell the cell from the light.

This is a reading of seven results in two species, not a law. A pass on our yardstick means within about a quarter of the spread between real animals, not identical. The evidence has every number and every caveat.
