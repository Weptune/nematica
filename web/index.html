What makes a real brain different from an artificial one?

Not by analogy. NEMATICA puts real nervous systems and artificial neural networks through the same tests, and judges both against one yardstick: how much real animals differ from each other.

See how we test it

The question

Artificial neural networks began as a sketch of the brain, and the two are now compared constantly. But most comparisons are analogies, or tests only one side can take.

We want differences that are measured, the same way, on both. That takes three things most comparisons lack: recordings of a real brain complete enough to model neuron by neuron, artificial networks recorded and analysed exactly like the animal, and a fair pass mark.

A brain small enough to see whole
The worm C. elegans: 302 neurons and a mapped wiring diagram. In a public atlas, 113 worms were recorded neuron by neuron while single neurons were switched on with light.
One pipeline for both
Artificial networks are recorded the way the worms were: same length, same missing neurons, same stimulation schedule, then analysed by the same code.
A pass mark made of individuals
No two worms are alike. A copy passes a test when it is as close to real worms as real worms are to each other: no closer, no further.

How we tackle it

The work runs as a protocol. Each step was written down before it was run, and every result, including the ones that went against us, is kept in the research log.

  1. Build a copy of a brain

    Fit models to the recordings: some learn only from the worm's ongoing activity, some also from what happened when single neurons were switched on. Every copy is tested on worms it never saw.

  2. Check the copy two ways: watch it, or poke it

    Watching means learning only from what a network does on its own. Poking means switching one neuron on and recording its echo, the response it spreads to the others. A faithful copy should predict the echo. Below, a model built only by watching each network predicts its echoes. Both networks have the same visible wiring; the one on the right also has links that stay silent at rest and carry signal only when a neuron is driven hard.

    each other neuron's measured echothe echo predicted by watchinga silent link carrying signal

    The score is the correlation between predicted and measured echoes: 1 is a perfect prediction, 0 is none.

  3. Judge it against real individuals

    Each copy is stimulated on the same schedule as a held-out worm, and its echoes are compared with real worms' echoes. The pass mark is the spread between real worms.

  4. Run the same test where the answer is known

    Artificial networks whose wiring we choose, untrained and trained on classic tasks, are recorded exactly like the worms and put through the unchanged pipeline. If the test fails them, the test is at fault; if it passes them and fails worms, the difference is real.

  5. Try to break our own result

    Build the worm experiment's own imperfections into the artificial networks: mislabelled neurons, neurons pooled into classes, stimulation that spreads to neighbouring cells. Then measure those imperfections in the real recordings, using every neuron's position.

  6. Ask other brains

    Repeat the question where single neurons or small groups can be stimulated: mouse hippocampus, mouse touch cortex, and people with implanted electrodes.

What we have found so far

Short versions, with how sure we are. The evidence page has the numbers, the figures and every caveat.

  • Found

    Looking right at rest says nothing about answering a poke. We scored 30 model brains on the same atlas worms twice: how real their resting activity looks, and how real their answers to single-neuron pokes are. The two scores are unrelated (rank correlation -0.12, p = 0.54). A linear model fitted to rest is indistinguishable from real worms on five of six activity statistics and answers pokes almost at random. A copy of a brain cannot be certified by watching it.

  • Found

    Each worm answers pokes in its own way, and its resting activity does not show it. A worm's responses to half of its pokes resemble its responses to the other half more than other worms' (36 of 43 worms, p = 5e-6). A model fitted to that worm's own rest predicts its responses no better than other worms' models do.

  • Found

    In worms, watching misses what poking finds. Copies built only by watching agree with real worms' echoes at 4% to 16% of the agreement between worms; copies that also learned from pokes reach the real-worm level. The strongest watcher we could build, a latent network fitted by maximum likelihood, does no better.

  • Found

    But being hard to copy by watching is not a brain-only property. Random and trained recurrent networks can largely be copied by watching (51% to 91%), yet networks given slow signals no recording sees fall to about 19%, near the worm, and trained transformers hide their cause and effect in the units that compute.

  • Found

    A worm neuron poked again soon answers about half as strongly. 0.6 to 2 minutes after its previous poke, a neuron's response is about half; after 2 to 5 minutes about two thirds; after 5 to 10 minutes it is not detectably weaker. Artificial networks answer the same way every time. The light-activated tool and the neuron cannot be separated in this dataset, but activity without light also lowers the next response a little, so part is probably the neuron. It persists in worms with reduced neuropeptide release. About 9% of the atlas's pokes are affected.

  • Found

    The worm's background state changes where a poke goes. Along the main pattern of a worm's resting activity, the same poke spreads differently in different states; replicated in a second set of worms and absent at matched moments without a poke. Artificial networks show it only with strong slow background signals. Beyond the worm it is not established.

  • By degree

    Brains change their rules over a recording; plain networks do not. How each unit's activity predicts the next moment drifts between the start and end of a recording in worms and in human cortex. Networks without slow processes, rate or spiking, keep exactly the same rules. Slow hidden signals or fatigue make networks drift too, but less than worms.

  • Withdrawn

    An echo that is narrow and lasting at once. This looked like the worm's most distinctive property until a control without any poke gave nearly the same values: the measure was reading the slow pace of ongoing activity. Against clean quiet moments, pokes lengthen responses in worms and networks alike.

  • Partly explained

    Part of the worm gap is the experiment. Stimulating one worm neuron also raises its nearest neighbours, by about a third of its own response. Built into artificial networks at that strength, it roughly halves what watching recovers. A gap remains.

  • Our reading

    The difference is what a system carries outside its units' activity. Every difference we found traces back to state a recording does not show: slow signals no neuron carries, changes left behind by use, switching between calm and active stretches. An ordinary artificial network's state is its units' activity; a brain's is more. Give a network that kind of hidden state and it acquires the worm's properties. This is a reading across the results, not yet a direct test.

  • Not detected

    Sensory neurons are not harder to copy by watching. We predicted they would be; the shares for sensory neurons, interneurons and motor neurons are too noisy to rank.

  • Open

    Whether it holds beyond the worm. In 74 people, a watching copy of the cortex predicts its responses worse than distance alone (0.30 against 0.45), which fits the worm; mouse data was inconclusive.

What would settle it

  1. Tire a neuron without light. Drive a worm neuron hard through its inputs, then poke it with light, against light followed by light at matched activity. That separates a tiring neuron from a tiring tool.
  2. Long, complete recordings, then pokes. Our calibration shows 16 minutes of rest is too short to measure slow hidden state; recordings of an hour or more, then stimulation of the same worm.
  3. The same neuron at several strengths. Links that stay silent at rest predict a threshold at the edge of a neuron's resting range.
