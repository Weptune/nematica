A copied brain can look alive and still be wrong.

NEMATICA tests artificial nervous systems against whole-brain recordings of living C. elegans. Each brain is watched at rest, then poked one neuron at a time, and every verdict is measured against how much real worms differ from one another.

Summary

People are building digital nervous systems, and sooner or later someone will claim that an artificial brain is a faithful copy of a real one. There is no agreed way to check that claim. We built one, on the only animal whose brain can be compared completely: the nematode C. elegans, with 302 neurons, every connection mapped, and every neuron in its head imaged while it lives.

The test treats an artificial brain the way a neurologist examines a patient who cannot speak. Watch it at rest. Perturb it. Read the echo, including with the measure that consciousness science uses to tell a responsive brain from an unresponsive one. Every score is expressed in units of how different real worms are from each other, so a brain passes a test when it is as close to real worms as they are to each other: no closer, and no further.

Key results

Watching a brain does not tell you how it works.

Looking alive is not enough

A brain that passes five of six tests of ongoing activity still gets cause and effect wrong. Across 88 brain scores, how alive a brain looks at rest says nothing about how it answers a poke (rank correlation −0.06).

Every worm has a causal fingerprint

A worm's echoes are repeatable and its own (36 of 43 worms), yet a model of that worm's resting activity predicts them no better than another worm's model does.

Real brains hold most neurons back

After a poke, a third of a real worm's neuron classes stay silent. Every artificial brain lets nearly all of them flicker in, which is why none matches a real echo's complexity.

Scorecard

Seventeen artificial brains, eleven tests, three groups of worms they never saw.

Simple models trained on ongoing activity pass most of the watching tests and fail every poking test. Building a brain that answers a poke like a real worm took four things together: causal links learned from pokes, ongoing dynamics learned from watching, fast measurement noise, and calm and active states. That brain is highlighted. Every fix so far has cost something elsewhere, and no brain passes everything.

The ruler

Inside means as different as two real worms are.

Two healthy worms never behave the same, and a good copy of a worm should not be more average than any real one. So each test asks where a brain falls among the distances between real worms. A brain that is closer to real worms than they are to each other is flagged as too typical, which catches models that have learned an average worm instead of a worm.

The poke test is narrow. A brain must carry a correlation of roughly 0.3 to 0.5 with the true average echo: less and it does not know enough cause and effect; more and it is more stereotyped than any worm.
