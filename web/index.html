What makes a real brain different from an artificial one?

Not by analogy. NEMATICA puts real nervous systems and artificial neural networks through the same tests, and judges both against one yardstick: how much real animals differ from each other.

The question

Artificial neural networks began as a sketch of the brain, and the two are now compared constantly. But most comparisons are analogies, or tests only one side can take.

We want differences that are measured, the same way, on both. That takes three things most comparisons lack: recordings of a real brain complete enough to model neuron by neuron, artificial networks recorded and analysed exactly like the animal, and a fair pass mark.

A brain small enough to see whole
The worm C. elegans: 302 neurons and a mapped wiring diagram. In a public atlas, 113 worms were recorded neuron by neuron while single neurons were switched on with light.
One pipeline for both
Artificial networks are recorded the way the worms were: same length, same missing neurons, same stimulation schedule, then analysed by the same code.
A pass mark made of individuals
No two worms are alike. A model passes a test when it is as close to real worms as real worms are to each other: no closer, no further.

How we tackle it

The work runs as a protocol. Each step was written down before it was run, and every result, including the ones that went against us, is kept in the research log.

  1. Build a copy of a brain

    Fit models to the recordings: some learn only from the worm's ongoing activity, some also from what happened when single neurons were switched on. Every copy is tested on worms it never saw.

  2. Check the copy two ways: watch it, or poke it

    Watching means learning only from what a network does on its own. Poking means switching one neuron on and recording its echo, the response it spreads to the others. A faithful copy should predict the echo. Below, a model built only by watching each network predicts its echoes. Both networks have the same visible wiring; the one on the right also has links that stay silent at rest and carry signal only when a neuron is driven hard.

    measured echo predicted by watching a silent link carrying signal. The score is the correlation between them, 1 for a perfect prediction.

  3. Judge it against real individuals

    Each copy is stimulated on the same schedule as a held-out worm, and its echoes are compared with real worms' echoes. The pass mark is the spread between real worms.

  4. Run the same test where the answer is known

    Artificial networks whose wiring we choose, untrained and trained on classic tasks, are recorded exactly like the worms and put through the unchanged pipeline. If the test fails them, the test is at fault; if it passes them and fails worms, the difference is real.

  5. Try to break our own result

    Build the worm experiment's own imperfections into the artificial networks: mislabelled neurons, neurons pooled into classes, stimulation that spreads to neighbouring cells. Then measure those imperfections in the real recordings, using every neuron's position.

  6. Ask other brains

    Repeat the question where single neurons or small groups can be stimulated: mouse hippocampus, mouse touch cortex, and people with implanted electrodes.

What we have found so far

Short versions, with how sure we are. The evidence page has the numbers, the figures and every caveat.

  • Found

    In worms, watching misses what poking finds. Copies built only by watching agree with real worms' echoes at 4% to 16% of the agreement between worms; copies that also learned from pokes reach the real-worm level.

  • Found

    Artificial networks give their workings away to an observer. Recorded and scored like worms, random networks are copied by watching at 51% to 82%, and networks trained on memory and decision tasks at 75% to 91%.

  • Partly explained

    Part of the worm gap is the experiment. Stimulating one worm neuron also raises its nearest neighbours, by about a third of its own response. Built into artificial networks at that strength, it roughly halves what watching recovers. A gap remains.

  • Open

    What explains the rest. Our lead: links that stay silent at rest, like the network on the right above. It is sufficient in artificial networks; whether worms have them is untested.

  • Open

    Whether it holds beyond the worm. Mouse hippocampus is inconclusive. In 74 people, watching does worse than simple distance, but the measurement must be redone with the stimulation pulse removed.

What would settle it

  1. Long, complete recordings, then pokes. Thirty minutes or more of a worm's whole brain at rest, every neuron identified, then stimulation of the same worm.
  2. The same neuron at several strengths. Silent links predict a threshold at the edge of a neuron's resting range.
  3. The cause of the neighbour effect. Neighbours that are truly activated should pass the signal on to their own partners; a recording artefact would not.
