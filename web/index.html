NEMATICA / ONE QUESTION, ASKED OPENLY

How would you know a brain copy is faithful? Will it answer like the real one?

One day someone will say an artificial brain is a copy of a real one. Nobody agrees how to check. NEMATICA is building that check, on the one brain small enough to compare completely, the 302 neurons of the worm C. elegans, and on artificial brains whose truth we know.

Research in progress. Part of the question is answered; the rest needs new data.

THE QUESTION

If someone hands you an artificial brain and says this is a copy of a real one, how would you check?

People are building digital nervous systems: brain models fitted to recordings, simulations built from wiring diagrams, AI networks trained to behave like animals. Sooner or later one of them will be called a faithful copy. Today the usual evidence is that the model reproduces recorded activity. We think that is not enough, and the work on this site is about finding out what is.

Underneath sits a second question, about artificial and real networks in general: what can you learn about a brain by watching it, and what only by intervening?

A yardstick made of individuals

No two real worms are alike. So a copy passes a test when it is as close to real worms as they are to each other: no closer (that would be an average, not a brain), and no further.

Two ways to know a brain

Watch it: record what it does on its own. Or poke it: switch on one neuron with light and see where the signal goes. A faithful copy should get both right. We test each, and test which one can teach a copy cause and effect.

Brains small enough to check completely

The worm C. elegans: 302 neurons, every connection mapped, 113 worms recorded while single neurons were poked. And artificial brains built so that their true cause and effect is known, so the test itself can be checked.

Where the question stands

  1. Answered

    Can a copy's faithfulness be measured at all?

    Yes, within limits we know. The test always catches a copy with half its cause and effect wrong, and lets through errors under about 20 to 25%. Checked on real worms with planted errors, and on artificial brains where the truth is known.

    How the test was checked
  2. Answered

    Does a copy that looks alive work like the real thing?

    No. Copies can pass most tests of ongoing activity and still answer a poke 4 to 100 times worse than one real worm answers like another. Looking alive does not predict answering right.

    The evidence
  3. Answered for the worm

    Which copies pass?

    Only copies that learned from pokes. Every kind of network that skipped them fails: fitted to recordings, built from the wiring diagram, randomly wired, trained on a worm's task, or published.

    Every network family
  4. Strong evidence

    Why does watching fail?

    Because what goes unrecorded, unseen neurons or slow signals shared across the brain, caps what watching can learn, however long you watch. Poking is not capped. Shown in worms and in artificial brains, and one prediction from it came true on real worms.

    The law
  5. Needs new data

    Can one particular brain be copied?

    Real worms have individual echoes, but no dataset pokes the same neuron enough times in one animal to build or check a copy of that animal.

    Why it is blocked
  6. Not yet

    Does a faithful copy carry over to behaviour and other situations?

    Nothing has transferred so far. Copies of freely moving worms fail the poke test, and poking the copper sensor in still worms does not predict how moving worms' brains answer copper.

    What did not transfer

THE LAW WE KEEP FINDING

Watching is capped by what you do not record. Poking is not.

To test this where the answer is known, we built artificial brains: nonlinear networks tuned so their responses are as noisy as real worms', with cause and effect known by construction. Then we tried to learn that cause and effect, by watching (with copies that are themselves neural networks) and by poking.

When every neuron is recorded and nothing hidden drives them, watching works well. Add slow signals that no recording sees, or record the way real experiments do, half the neurons at a time, and every watching copy falls short, most of them levelling off within hours, while poking keeps climbing past them.

Taking the hidden signals out of a watching copy rescues it only when every neuron is recorded (0.61 to 0.77). Recorded like the atlas, it changes nothing (0.30 to 0.30). On real worms, as predicted, it changed nothing too.

What we found

Discoveries so far, including the ones that went against us.

Looking alive is not working right

A network with randomly dealt connections passes nearly as many tests of ongoing activity (2 to 4 of 6) as one fitted to real worms (3 to 5). Those tests cannot certify cause and effect.

Only pokes teach cause and effect

Every family of artificial network that never saw a poke answers one 4 to 100 times worse than real worms answer each other. Only copies that learned from pokes reach the real-worm level.

The same law in artificial brains

In networks whose truth is known, every watching copy falls short, including neural networks with memory; poking passes all of them by 40 hours of recording in every setting.

The test, checked against the truth

On a species of artificial individuals, the unchanged test rejects badly wrong copies and certifies copies within about 20% of an individual. It also repeats the worm verdicts: the watching copy fails, the poke-trained copy mostly passes.
−0.01how much meeting copper resembles poking ASH, the copper sensor

Nothing transfers across contexts

Copies learned from freely moving worms answer pokes at chance. The echo of poking ASH in still worms does not resemble moving worms' brains meeting copper; no poked neuron's echo does better than chance.
160+worms poking one neuron for a reliable echo; the atlas has 22

Not peptides, and pokes are noisy

We predicted the hidden signals were neuropeptides. Wrong: worms that release far fewer show the same slow shared activity. And one worm's echo agrees with another's at only 0.07, so reliable echoes need many more pokes.
26 of 44worms where watching every tracked neuron beat watching only the named ones

Recording more of a worm helps, a little

The direction the explanation predicts, but the gain is tiny: each worm has about 3 usable minutes of unpoked activity, and other worms' pokes still predict it far better. Existing data cannot settle it.

WHAT WOULD ANSWER THE REST

We have reached the edge of the data that exists.

A prediction, written down before the experiment exists

Record a worm's whole brain, with nearly every neuron identified, for 30 to 60 minutes before poking it. Then a copy that only watched, with the slow shared signals taken out, should come close to predicting that same worm's pokes.

If it does, brain copies could be checked without poking every neuron. If it does not, our explanation is wrong. Today a worm offers about 3 usable minutes of unpoked activity: adding its unnamed neurons helps a little (better in 26 of 44 worms), far too little data to decide.

  1. Complete, fully identified recordings in worms that are then poked.
  2. Repeated pokes of the same neurons in the same worm, to copy one individual.
  3. Many more pokes per neuron, aimed where echoes are least reliable.
  4. Pokes in freely moving worms, with what the worm is doing recorded.
  5. Reporters for the slow shared signals, to find what carries them.

Scorecard

Seventeen artificial brains, eleven tests, three groups of worms they never saw.

Simple models trained on ongoing activity pass most of the watching tests and fail every poking test. Building a brain that answers a poke like a real worm took four things together: causal links learned from pokes, ongoing dynamics learned from watching, fast measurement noise, and calm and active states. That brain is highlighted. Every fix so far has cost something elsewhere, and no brain passes everything. This table was computed before the 1 November data repair, in which three truncated recordings were rebuilt from the original archive; the headline brains were rerun on the repaired data and their results held.

The ruler

Inside means as different as two real worms are.

Two healthy worms never behave the same, and a good copy of a worm should not be more average than any real one. So each test asks where a brain falls among the distances between real worms. A brain that is closer to real worms than they are to each other is flagged as too typical, which catches models that have learned an average worm instead of a worm.

The poke test is narrow. A brain must carry a correlation of roughly 0.3 to 0.5 with the true average echo: less and it does not know enough cause and effect; more and it is more stereotyped than any worm.

We measured how sharp that is by putting known errors into real worms' own responses. The test never flags real worms, always catches a copy that credits the wrong neuron for half its pokes or gets response size wrong by half, and often misses errors of about a quarter. A pass means within about 25% of a real worm on these tests, not a faithful copy.

We then checked the test where the truth is known, on a species of artificial individuals: in species whose echoes are as reliable as worms', it rejects copies with a quarter of their connections moved in every group and certifies copies within about 20% of an individual. It also has limits we found that way: when individuals differ a lot, or hidden signals dominate, data the size of the atlas cannot certify anything, and with much more data the agreement rule fixed on 1 November becomes too lenient and must be tightened.
