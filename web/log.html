The research log.

Can you tell a copied brain from a real one? This log keeps the question, the prediction, the result and the miss together, one experiment at a time.

Read the research log ↓

  1. Arc 1

    Forecasting is not fidelity

    Each worm gets its own model, pulled toward a shared one by a tunable penalty. The setting that forecasts best makes simulated worms too typical: they are closer to every real worm than real worms are to each other. A setting that forecasts almost as well (0.009 lower) sits right on the real-worm line. Good prediction and a faithful copy are different targets.

    0.009lower forecasting skill buys a model that sits on the real-worm line

  2. Generalisation

    Does a model learn worms, or one dataset?

    Models trained on one set of recordings were scored against another lab's worms. The two sets of real worms differ from each other in ways the ruler can see, and the calibrations replicated in full (44 of 44). The models replicated in part: inside on 5 or 6 of 10 tests with every setting carried over unchanged.

    44 of 44calibration outcomes replicated on the second dataset

  3. Arc 3

    What the wiring buys

    Restricting a model's connections to the anatomical wiring diagram, with everything else identical. Given the same number of connections, the real wiring beats random wiring, consistently. But any wiring constraint makes the model less realistic than letting the data choose the coupling. Anatomy carries real but small information about how these neurons drive each other.

    Smallbut real: the anatomical wiring beats random wiring of the same density
    Full record

  4. Arc 5

    A worm that senses copper

    A brain model connected to a simulated body in each real worm's own arena, with copper fed back as the body moves. The first version spent 40% of its time near copper, against 3% for real worms. By the eleventh version, with copper entering the brain only through the sensory neuron ASH, simulated worms are inside on 9 of 10 activity tests and all three behaviour tests. It still spends more time near copper than real worms do (13% against 3%).

    9 of 10activity tests, and all three behaviour tests, for the eleventh version

  5. Arc 4, the headline

    The causal Turing test

    Watch a brain, then poke it. Brains that pass the watching tests fail the poke tests, and passing our rest checks has not reliably identified good poke responses. Each worm has its own causal fingerprint. One hybrid brain now passes the poke test in all three held-out groups; none yet matches the echo's complexity while staying realistic at rest.

    −0.06rest-score/poke-distance correlation across 88 model-group scores; variants repeat across groups
    Read the evidence

  6. Signatures of a mind

    Measures from consciousness science

    At rest, simple models match real worms on complexity, integration and repertoire of brain states, on two datasets. Under perturbation, the echo's complexity separates every model from real worms: real brains keep a third of their neuron classes out of each echo, and models let almost all of them flicker in.

    66%of a real worm’s neuron classes join an echo; models let in over 90%

  7. Arc 6

    From one brain to a swarm

    Many simulated worms in one arena, touching and avoiding each other. Does a crowd of identical average worms behave differently from a crowd of distinct individuals? The earlier exported run crossed its intended copper escape limit. A live behavioral sandbox now contains the worms and exposes the contact rule, but its crowd statistics are not yet evidence about real swarms; the mixed-versus-clone difference is not established across seeds.

  8. 1 Nov 2026 · Data

    Three recordings were broken, and are repaired

    Rebuilding the stimulated-worm data from the original archive with a script anyone can run showed that 3 of the 113 recordings had been cut short when the archive was unpacked, and had been scored as about a minute of scrambled data each. The other 110 rebuild exactly. The headline brains were rerun on the repaired data: 16 of 132 verdicts changed, almost all on activity tests near the boundary, and the poke-pattern result held.

  9. 1 Nov 2026 · Gallery

    Every kind of network that skipped pokes fails

    Networks fitted to recordings, randomly wired, built from the wiring diagram, trained on a worm task with or without the real wiring, and a published model with its own stimulation: on six held-out groups, all answer a poke four to a hundred times worse than real worms answer each other. Only networks that learned from pokes reach the real-worm level, and on a stricter test fixed in advance the best of them is shown to agree in all six groups.

    6 of 6groups where the brain that learned from pokes is shown to agree; the brain that watched, 0
    Read the evidence

  10. 1 Nov 2026 · Why

    Watching hits a ceiling that more data does not lift

    In synthetic worms whose cause and effect is known, watching learns it until a few slow, shared, unrecorded signals cap it within hours; poking keeps improving and overtakes it at about the atlas's size. On real worms, a watching copy is no better after 58 worms than after 6, while pokes from 6 worms already beat it four to seven times over.

    0.20ceiling on what watching recovers, at 2.5 hours and at 70
    Read the evidence

  11. 1 Nov 2026 · Resolution

    How sharp the test is

    Errors of known size put into real worms: no false alarms, a copy that credits the wrong neuron for half its pokes or gets response size wrong by half is always caught, and errors of about a quarter often pass. A pass means within about 25% of a real worm on these tests.

    25%the size of error the test often misses

  12. 1 Nov 2026 · Individuals

    Can one particular worm be copied?

    A worm's echoes agree with themselves twice as well as with other worms, but one recording barely beats the average worm at predicting them: the atlas rarely pokes the same neuron twice in one animal. Worms that cannot release neuropeptides still look fully real (63 of 66 verdicts).

    +0.007what a worm's own first half adds over the average worm
    Read the evidence

  13. 2 Nov 2026 · Why

    No sign that the hidden signals are neuropeptides

    We predicted watching would do better in worms with neuropeptide release much reduced, the obvious suspect for the shared signals that defeat it. The prediction failed: watching stays near zero, and the shared slow structure is as strong as in normal worms, about seven patterns with a timescale near 90 seconds. A synthetic worm given slow drives of that shape still shows the ceiling; if they are weak, poking needs more than 70 hours to overtake watching.

    0.009echo agreement of a watching copy with neuropeptide release reduced (normal worms: 0.017)
    Read the evidence

  14. 2 Nov 2026 · Design

    How many pokes a lab needs

    Exploratory. One worm's echo agrees with another's at only 0.07, and averaging helps slowly. Pinning one neuron's echo down takes at least about 160 worms poking it, against a median of 22 in the atlas. The feeding organ's own neurons give echoes three times as reproducible as the rest.

    160+worms per neuron for a reliable echo
    Read the evidence

  15. 2 Nov 2026 · Context

    Copies of moving worms fail the poke test, and copper is not like poking its sensor

    Copies fitted by watching freely moving worms predict their activity well and answer pokes at about chance. In the other direction, the brain-wide response of moving worms to copper does not resemble the echo of poking the copper sensor in still worms. One stimulus, so a warning rather than a law.

    36th of 74where the copper sensor's echo ranks in matching the copper response
    Read the evidence

  16. 2 Nov 2026 · Artificial lab

    The same test on brains whose truth is known

    Nonlinear artificial brains, tuned so that their single-poke echoes are as noisy as real worms', with cause and effect known by construction. Copies that learned by watching, including neural networks with memory, stop short; averaging pokes passes all of them by 40 hours of recording in every setting. Run on a species of artificial individuals, the unchanged ruler rejects badly wrong copies, certifies copies within about 20% of an individual, and repeats the worm verdicts: the watching copy fails, the poke-trained copy mostly passes. When individuals differ a lot or hidden drives dominate, atlas-sized data cannot certify anything.

    0.62best any watching copy reached with slow hidden drives; poking 0.82
    Read the evidence

  17. 2 Nov 2026 · Data

    Where the data runs out

    In artificial brains, taking the hidden drives out of a watching copy works only when every neuron is recorded: from 0.61 to 0.77 with everything recorded, no change with atlas-like recording, and on real worms no change either. What is left needs new experiments: complete, fully identified recordings in worms that are then poked; repeated pokes of the same neurons in one worm; many more pokes per neuron; pokes in freely moving worms; and reporters for whatever carries the slow shared signals.

  18. 2 Nov 2026 · Individuals

    Recording more of one worm helps watching, a little

    The atlas names about 72 neurons per worm but tracks about 123. Within single worms, a watching copy that uses every tracked neuron beats one limited to named neurons in 26 of 44 worms, as the explanation predicts, but by very little, and other worms' pokes still predict each worm far better. With about 3 usable unpoked minutes per worm, existing data cannot settle it.

  19. 3 Nov 2026 · Artificial lab, correction

    Much of the watching ceiling was our method; worms are still different

    Every watching copy we had built filled the neurons a recording missed with zeros. Built to learn each pair of neurons only from recordings where both were seen, with slow drift removed, a watching copy of artificial brains recorded like the atlas climbs from 0.34 to 0.79, close to drift-corrected poking at 0.85. Scored exactly as worms are, it reaches 62% to 82% of how well individuals agree, beside 75% to 89% for the poke-trained copy. On real worms the same copies reach 4% to 22%, the poke-trained copy 76% to 97%. So worms are far harder to copy by watching than these artificial brains, and the artificial brains no longer explain why. An independent review of a first draft caught an unfair comparison with poking, which is corrected here. Earlier entries that call the ceiling a law are kept as written.

    62–82%watching, artificial brains scored like worms; real worms 4–22%

  20. 3 Nov 2026 · Mouse hippocampus

    The first test beyond the worm: not sharp enough yet

    In mouse CA1, in the 16 sessions that passed every pre-registered check, how cells moved with a stimulated interneuron while only watched ordered their responses to stimulation weakly (about 0.15), where distance ordered nothing. A sham control, at times when nothing was poked, gives a smaller but similar pattern, and the difference is not significant, so this does not yet show that watching predicts pokes here. An independent review caught a duplicated-stimulation bug that had inflated the ceiling; it is fixed, and every change made after pre-registration is listed.

  21. 3 Nov 2026 · Artificial lab, exploratory

    Looking for why worms resist watching

    We built candidate explanations into artificial brains and scored each exactly as worms are. A slow calcium indicator changed nothing. Worm-like smooth, low-dimensional resting activity left them easy to copy by watching. Only connections that are silent at rest but carry most of a poke's response brought watching down to near the worms' level (19% to 28% of individuals' agreement, against 4% to 22% in worms). This is a lead about what is possible, not evidence about worms; the review that checked it asked for that wording and for more controls. The first, adding the same silent links to brains whose resting activity looks like a worm's, gives the same pattern (watching 19% to 28%, poke-trained 93% to 96%).

    19–28%watching, artificial brains with silent links; worms 4–22%
    Read the evidence

  22. 3 Nov 2026 · Checks

    The headline survives two hard checks

    A copy that answers every poke with the average response to any poke, knowing that something was poked but not what, fails the test as badly as watching copies do, so the test measures which neuron causes what, not a stereotyped response to stimulation. A standalone recomputation with independent code gives the same order: watching does a fifth to a quarter as well as other worms' pokes. We also simulated the experiment that could test our lead about silent pathways: it needs many neurons each stimulated at several strengths, with 20 or more trials per strength.

    0.001–0.012a copy that knows only that something was poked; poke-trained 0.08–0.09
    Read the evidence

  23. 4 Nov 2026 · Human brains, interim

    In people, watching predicts stimulation responses worse than distance does

    An early look at the pre-registered human test, on 100 of 117 recordings from 63 epilepsy patients with electrodes on the brain's surface. A copy built from the activity between stimulation pulses predicts each patient's own responses less well than simple distance from the stimulated site, in 62 of 63 patients, and less well than other patients' measured responses, in 50 of 63. Both predictions written down beforehand hold. The final result on every recording, and its independent review, follow when the download finishes.

    0.28watching copy; distance 0.47; other patients 0.37; ceiling 0.70
    How sure we are

  24. 4 Nov 2026 · Human brains, final

    The human result holds as computed, but the measurement is partly artefact

    On all 74 patients (the last recording downloaded a day later and changed nothing material) the numbers matched the interim look: the watching copy predicted each patient's responses worse than distance (in 73 of 74 patients) and worse than other patients' measured responses (56 of 74). The independent review then found that the measured "response" partly contains the electrical artefact of the stimulation pulse itself, spread by our filtering: it is present even before the pulse and falls off with distance. So distance looks strong partly because it predicts the artefact, and we do not claim anything about human cortex until the recordings are re-extracted with the pulse blanked out. The watching copy was weak but not broken: it reproduced how responses fall off with distance and added a little beyond it.

    56 of 74patients where other people's pokes beat the watching copy
    How sure we are

  25. 5 Nov 2026 · Silent pathways, tested in worms

    Pokes do push neurons past their resting range; the decisive comparison could not be made

    Our best explanation for why worms resist watching is pathways that stay silent until a neuron is pushed beyond the range it visits at rest. We pre-registered a test in the worm data. Its first part holds: in 64% of stimulations the target goes beyond its resting range (6% would by chance). Its second part, whether a unit of drive does more beyond that range, failed its check on artificial brains, so it says nothing either way. An exploratory follow-up found that in worms, stimulations after which the stimulated neuron shows no measurable change at all are still followed by responses in other neurons. The independent review showed that most of this response is not specific to the stimulated neuron, so it does not yet reveal a hidden pathway; light reaching neighbouring cells or misidentified neurons could explain it.

    64%of worm pokes push the target beyond its resting range
    How sure we are

  26. 5 Nov 2026 · Trained networks

    Training a network on a task does not make it hide its cause and effect

    Our artificial brains so far were random, untrained networks, and those can be copied by watching. The networks people usually mean by "artificial neural networks" are trained. We trained the same kind of network on two classic tasks (holding three bits in memory, and integrating noisy evidence depending on context) and put each one, and the identical network before training, through the exact worm pipeline. All four can be copied by watching: the trained ones reach 75% to 91% of what another individual of the same network achieves, against 4% to 22% for worms. So the difference between worms and these artificial networks is not simply that worms are trained by evolution and development. The next test builds the worm experiment's own imperfections (misidentified neurons, pooled classes, light reaching neighbours, short rest) into the artificial networks to see whether any of them recreates the worm gap.

    75 to 91%trained networks copied by watching (worms: 4 to 22%)
    How sure we are

  27. 5 Nov 2026 · Biology or experiment?

    One of the worm experiment's flaws could explain the gap; testing whether it is big enough

    Before calling "worms hide their cause and effect from an observer" a difference between biological and artificial networks, we had to rule out the worm experiment itself. We built its flaws into artificial networks: mislabelled neurons, left and right neurons pooled together, and stimulation spreading to neighbouring cells. Strong spread did recreate the worm pattern, so we measured it in the real worms, using the 3D positions of every neuron in the original archive. It is there: a stimulated neuron's nearest neighbours carry about 0.3 of its response, only when it responds, for reasons we cannot yet identify. We first concluded that this measured spread was far too weak to matter. An independent review showed that our comparison was not like for like: measured over the same 15 seconds the test uses, the worms' spread sits between two strengths we had not run, one that leaves artificial networks easy to watch and one that makes them worm-like. Those runs are under way. Until they finish, stimulation spread is the leading experimental explanation of the worm gap, and the claim that worms differ from artificial networks in their biology waits on it.

    0.3of a stimulated neuron's response carried by its nearest neighbours in worms; whether that is enough is being tested
    How sure we are

  28. 5 Nov 2026 · How much does the spread explain?

    Stimulation spread explains a large part of the worm gap, not all of it

    We ran the artificial networks with stimulation spreading to neighbours at the strengths that bracket what the worms show. Watching copies, which recover 63% to 72% without spread, fall to 27% to 49% with it: about half the gap to the worms' 4% to 22%. If the worms' spread is the light really activating neighbouring cells, a large part of why worms resist watching is the experiment; if it is a quirk of how the neurons were tracked, little of it is. Telling these apart is the next test: really activated neighbours should pass the signal on to their own partners.

    27–49%watching in artificial networks with the worms' spread (no spread: 63–72%; worms: 4–22%)
    How sure we are
