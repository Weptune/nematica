FINDINGS

Looking like a brain and working like one are different things.

Every finding here answers part of one question: how would anyone check that an artificial brain is a faithful copy of a real one? We fitted artificial brains to living worms and tested them on worms they had never seen, by watching and by switching single neurons on with light, then repeated the test in artificial brains whose truth is known. Predictions were written down before the experiments ran, exploratory results are labelled, and the results that went against us are kept.

A brain can pass the watching tests and fail every poke.

The obvious way to check an artificial brain is to compare its activity with a real one's: are the values, rhythms and correlations right? We ran that check and a harder one on the same 113 worms. A linear model fitted to the worms' ongoing activity passes three to five of six activity statistics in each of six held-out groups of worms. When one of its neurons is switched on with light, its echo agrees with real worms at 0.004 to 0.014; two real worms agree with each other at 0.087 to 0.121. A brain that also learned from pokes reaches 0.088 to 0.113, and on a stricter test, fixed in advance, that asks for agreement within a stated tolerance rather than merely no difference detected, its echo pattern is shown to match real worms in all six groups. The brain that only watched: in none.

That might be one unlucky model, so we scored every brain we built on both. In 88 model-group scores, the number of watching tests passed had a weak association with echo agreement (rank correlation about +0.09). Using the pre-specified continuous rest score instead, the correlation with poke-pattern distance was −0.06 (p = 0.56). The 88 scores include repeated results from 30 model variants across held-out groups, so this does not prove that rest and poke performance are unrelated in general. It shows that passing these watching tests does not reliably identify the models that answer pokes correctly.

Every kind of network that skipped pokes fails, whatever it is.

Is the failure about the linear model, or about something deeper? We put every family of artificial network we could build through the same test, on six held-out groups of worms: networks fitted to recordings; the same network with its connections dealt out at random; the classic model built from the wiring diagram with textbook physiology and fitted to nothing; networks trained to do a worm's job, steering towards food and away from harm, with or without the real wiring as a constraint and never shown a neural recording; and a published model fitted to this very atlas, run with its own stimulation model.

Only networks that learned from pokes answer a poke like a worm. Everything else falls four to a hundred times short, in every group. Among networks that never saw a poke, structure helps a little: the wiring diagram gets the most, a network that watched gets less, and the same network with random wiring gets nothing. The tests of ongoing activity do not put these networks in order: no family passes all six in any group, and the randomly wired network passes nearly as many as the one fitted to real worms.

Why: watching cannot see past shared signals it does not record.

A real worm cannot tell us its true cause and effect, so a model brain stands in: a published model of 154 neurons in which ongoing activity and responses to pokes come from the same dynamics. We record it the way the atlas was recorded, with half the neurons seen per recording, some never seen, and measurement noise, and try to learn its cause and effect from the recordings.

Watching works in principle. When each neuron's fluctuations are its own, it recovers cause and effect well, better than poking at the atlas's size. But add a few slow, shared, brain-wide signals that no recording captures, at a level that matches real resting worms, and watching hits a ceiling within a few hours that more data never lifts. Poking is not fooled by those signals: it keeps improving and overtakes watching between 40 and 70 hours of recording, the size of the atlas. A second published model as ground truth gives the same picture, and removing the strongest shared patterns before learning does not reliably help.

The real worms behave as predicted. A copy that learns by watching is no better after watching 58 worms than after 6 (echo agreement 0.004 to 0.014 throughout), while a copy given the pokes of just 6 worms already beats it four to seven times over, and keeps improving with more worms.

What this would mean. If real brains are driven by strong shared signals that recordings miss, a brain cannot be copied by watching it, however long. Intervention is then not a refinement but a necessity. These stand-in brains are linear and were themselves fitted to this atlas; the nonlinear test that follows confirms the ceiling and refines the explanation.

Where the truth is known, watching comes close to poking, and the test can be checked.

Real worms cannot tell us their true cause and effect, so we built artificial brains that can: nonlinear networks of 120 units, tuned so that two individuals' single-poke echoes agree about as well as two worms' do, with cause and effect known by construction. Recordings copy the atlas's design.

Watching versus poking. When every unit is recorded and nothing hidden drives them, a neural network that watches recovers cause and effect well (0.82), though poking still passes it with enough recording (0.91). Add slow hidden signals and none of these watching copies passes 0.62, however it is poked or however much memory it has, while poking reaches 0.82. Recorded like the atlas, half the units at a time, the watching copies stay at about 0.5 or below and poking reaches 0.65 to 0.88 by 40 hours. Poking passes every one of these watching copies by 40 hours of recording in every setting. (These copies all fill unrecorded units with zeros; a better-built copy, below, does much better.)

Taking the hidden signals out. Given the strongest slow patterns of its own recordings, a watching copy recovers most of what the hidden signals cost, from 0.61 to 0.77, when every unit is recorded. Recorded like the atlas, the same step changed nothing (0.30 to 0.30), and even the true hidden signals lifted it only to 0.48 against 0.76 for poking. We read this as a limit of the data. It was a limit of the copy.

Much of that was the copy. Every watching copy above fills the units a recording missed with zeros, so it learns from only part of its inputs. A copy that learns each pair of units only from the recordings where both were seen, with each unit's slow drift removed, changes the picture. Recorded like the atlas with slow hidden signals, it reaches 0.79 (from 0.34), against 0.85 for poking with the same drift correction; with less data than that it can lead (10 hours of each: 0.71 against 0.59), and with signals as fast as the responses it trails further (0.59 against 0.76). Built better, watching comes close to poking in artificial brains, but does not overtake it when poking has comparable data.

Scored exactly as worms are, the difference stays. Artificial individuals recorded like atlas worms (113 of them, about 2 minutes unpoked, then a poke every 30 seconds) and scored with the unchanged test: the better watching copy reaches 62% to 82% of how well individuals agree, beside 75% to 89% for the poke-trained copy, and passes in two of three groups when the hidden signals are strong. On real worms, built and scored the same way, watching copies reach 4% to 22% and the poke-trained copy 76% to 97%. Real worms are far harder to copy by watching than these artificial brains, and not because of the recording design, the amount of unpoked data or the score. Untested candidates: pokes drive neurons far beyond the range they visit at rest, waking links that are silent in ongoing activity; worm activity is dominated by a few global behavioural states; worm neurons respond nonlinearly in ways these units do not.

The test, checked against the truth. A species of 60 artificial individuals, each recorded once like an atlas worm, split three ways as the atlas is, and scored with the unchanged test. Copies of known quality: the species-average brain, another individual, and the species brain with 10%, 25% or all of its connections moved; plus the two copies we built for worms. Where echoes are as reliable as worms', the test separates good copies from bad ones as the truth does, rejects every copy with a quarter of its connections moved, and certifies copies within about 20% of an individual. The watching copy reaches 48% to 70% of an individual's closeness and fails; the poke-trained copy reaches 80% to 90% and mostly passes, as on real worms.

The check also found limits. The test cannot rank close copies correctly (it put the poke-trained copy above a genuine individual where the truth puts it below). When individuals differ a lot, or hidden signals dominate, data the size of the atlas cannot certify anything. And with twice the individuals, the agreement rule fixed on 1 November becomes lenient enough to certify the watching copy in two of three groups, so it must be tightened before any larger dataset is scored.

On the data we have, nothing transfers across contexts.

Four more tests use every dataset we have: worms with neuropeptide release much reduced, freely moving worms, and worms meeting a copper boundary.

No sign that the hidden signals are neuropeptides. They spread through the body without the wiring, so they were the obvious suspect, and we predicted watching would do better in worms that release far fewer of them. It did not: watching stays near zero (0.009 against 0.017 in normal worms, in the same small design), and the shared slow structure is just as strong, about seven shared patterns with a timescale near 90 seconds in both strains. Watching sits at its floor in both, so a small gain could hide there. A synthetic worm given slow drives of this shape still shows the ceiling, but when those drives are weak, poking needs more than the atlas's 70 hours to overtake watching.

How many pokes a lab needs (exploratory). One worm's echo agrees with another's at only 0.07, and averaging helps more slowly than textbook noise would. Projecting from the largest groups we can measure, pinning one neuron's echo down takes at least about 160 worms poking it, against a median of 22 in the atlas. The feeding organ's own small nervous system gives echoes three times as reproducible as the rest; beyond it there is no clean rule.

Copies learned from freely moving worms fail the poke test. Fitted by watching 27 to 57 moving worms, they predict the next moment of activity well and answer a poke at about chance, like every other watching copy.

Meeting copper is not like poking its sensor. In moving worms, the brain-wide response to the copper boundary does not resemble the echo of poking ASH, the copper sensor, in still worms (r = −0.01), and no poked neuron's echo matches it better than chance would. This is one stimulus, and copper reaches other sensors too, so it is a warning rather than a law.

What this would mean. A faithful copy needs pokes, the brain state they were given in, and the context the copy will be used in. We know of no public dataset with all three for the same worms.

The failure is about cause and effect, not noise.

A fair objection: maybe a model knows the right causal structure, and each single echo is just buried in the wrong kind of variability. If so, averaging many simulated repeats of the experiment should uncover it. It does not. Even ten full repeats averaged give an echo that agrees with the atlas average less than one real worm does. The published model fitted to this atlas comes closest, and still falls short.

Every worm has a causal fingerprint that watching does not reveal.

If real worms all answered pokes the same way, the failures above would just mean the models are imprecise. They do not. Split each worm's pokes into two halves: the two halves of the same worm agree with each other better than with other worms, in 36 of 43 worms (p = 5 × 10−6). The effect is not a recording-day artefact; worms recorded on the same day are no closer than worms from other days.

So we asked the sharper question. Fit a model to one worm's own resting activity. Does it predict that same worm's echoes better than models of other worms do? No: own model closer in 26 of 44 worms, median difference −0.008, p = 0.11. Each brain has its own wiring of cause and effect, and watching that brain at rest does not tell you what it is.

On the repaired data, with responses in units of each neuron's fluctuations, a worm's two halves agree at 0.17 and different worms at 0.09, across 94 worms, and the gap holds when the halves are the first and second half of the recording. Two explanations remain open: genuine individuality, or worm-specific measurement such as neuron identities assigned once per worm. Can one particular worm then be copied from its own recording? Barely: predicting a worm's second half from a blend of its own first half and the average worm beats the average worm alone by 0.007. In the atlas a pulse mostly hits a new neuron, about 1.8 pulses per stimulated class per worm, so copying one animal needs repeated pokes of the same neurons in the same worm. That is the experiment we have asked for.

What it took to answer a poke like a worm.

Nonlinear neurons, sparse wiring and training directly on pokes each failed on their own. The first brain to pass the poke test, with its echo pattern and spread inside in all three held-out groups (and, on the repaired data, shown to agree on the echo pattern within a tolerance fixed in advance in all six), needed four things together: causal links learned from pokes as links rather than as one-step dynamics; ongoing dynamics learned by watching; fast noise that does not propagate; and a brain that alternates between calm and active stretches.

The pass replicates: on a second, independently drawn split of the same worms, its echo pattern and spread are again inside in all three held-out groups. It is not the end of the story. That brain is weaker at rest than the plain linear model (three or four watching tests of six, against five), and its echoes are still too complex. Each fix we tried since moved one property toward real worms and pushed another away.

In a real brain, most of the brain stays out of the echo.

Consciousness research judges unresponsive patients by the complexity of the brain's echo to a magnetic pulse: the perturbational complexity index, which separates wakefulness from deep sleep, anaesthesia and coma. The optogenetic atlas is the same experiment in a worm, thousands of times over. Applied there, it is the one property no artificial brain matches yet.

We expected models to fail by being too simple. They fail the other way: their echoes are more complex than real ones. After a poke, a third of a real worm's neuron classes never respond within 30 seconds, and the ones that do keep responding. In every model, almost every class flickers across the threshold at some point, briefly. The restlessness comes from the models' ongoing dynamics rather than their causal links, which is why it survived every fix to the links.

Not a claim about consciousness. Nothing here says a worm or a model is conscious. These are the measurable properties that, in people, separate a responsive brain from an unresponsive one.

At rest, the same brains pass the consciousness-science measures.

Consciousness science has three standard measures of ongoing activity: how complex it is, how integrated its parts are, and how rich its repertoire of whole-brain states is. We expected linear models to come out too irregular. They do not: per-worm linear brains match real worms on all three, on two independent datasets. Measures of ongoing activity, however principled, cannot tell these copies from the real thing. The echo can.

Where the data runs out.

Everything that can be done with existing data has now been tried at least once. The last test asked whether recording more of one worm helps it be copied by watching. The atlas names a median of 72 neurons per worm but tracks about 123, and the unnamed ones are recorded, only not identifiable across worms. Within single worms, a watching copy that includes them does slightly better than one with named neurons only (better in 26 of 44 worms), and taking out slow patterns adds nothing. But each worm offers only about 3 usable minutes of unpoked activity, and the average of other worms' pokes predicts each worm's own echoes far better (0.22 against 0.05) in 43 of 44 worms.

What would answer the rest, in order of what it would unlock:

  1. Whole-brain recordings with nearly every neuron identified, 30 to 60 minutes long, in worms that are then poked. If missing neurons are why watching fails in worms, a well-built watching copy should then come close to predicting that worm's own pokes. If it does not, worms differ from our artificial brains in a deeper way.
  2. Repeated pokes of the same neurons in the same worm, to build and check a copy of one individual.
  3. Many more pokes per neuron, about 160 worms each for a reliable echo, aimed where echoes are least reliable.
  4. Pokes in freely moving worms, with the worm's behaviour recorded.
  5. Reporters for whatever carries the slow shared signals.

What this does not show.

  • It does not show that any model, or any worm, is conscious.
  • Neuron classes, not single neurons, are the unit throughout; left and right partners are averaged.
  • Calcium imaging is slow and indirect. A brain that matches these recordings matches what the microscope can see.
  • The brain that passes the poke test was confirmed on a second, independently drawn split of the worms (echo pattern and spread inside in all three new groups). Both splits come from one lab's recordings.
  • The test has a measured resolution: it never flags real worms, always catches errors of half, and often misses errors of about a quarter. A pass means within about 25% of a real worm on these tests.
  • On 1 November three of the 113 recordings were found to have been cut short when the archive was unpacked, and were rebuilt. Results marked as rerun use the repaired data; older figures are labelled.
  • The explanation of why watching fails rests on synthetic and artificial brains, linear and nonlinear, and on one prediction confirmed in real worms. What the hidden shared signals are in real worms is unknown; neuropeptides are not the main candidate.
  • Nothing learned in still, immobilised worms has been shown to carry over to freely moving ones.
  • No brain passes everything; each fix so far has cost something elsewhere.
