FINDINGS

Looking like a brain and working like one are different things.

Every finding here answers part of one question: what makes biological neural networks measurably different from artificial ones? Our tool is a test of copies, run the same way on both: we fitted artificial brains to living worms and tested them on worms they had never seen, by watching and by switching single neurons on with light, then repeated the test on artificial networks whose truth is known, untrained and trained on tasks, then took the comparison to mouse visual cortex and seven families of artificial network, from recurrent and spiking networks to transformers, each recorded through the animal's own instrument, and finally switched single kinds of cell on with light in 39 more mice. Predictions were written down before the experiments ran, exploratory results are labelled, and the results that went against us are kept.

Part one

The worm: copies of a brain, watched and poked

A brain can pass the watching tests and fail every poke.

The obvious way to check an artificial brain is to compare its activity with a real one's: are the values, rhythms and correlations right? We ran that check and a harder one on the same 113 worms. A linear model fitted to the worms' ongoing activity passes three to five of six activity statistics in each of six held-out groups of worms. When one of its neurons is switched on with light, its echo agrees with real worms at 0.004 to 0.014; two real worms agree with each other at 0.087 to 0.121. A brain that also learned from pokes reaches 0.088 to 0.113, and on a stricter test, fixed in advance, that asks for agreement within a stated tolerance rather than merely no difference detected, its echo pattern is shown to match real worms in all six groups. The brain that only watched: in none.

That might be one unlucky model, so we scored every brain we built on both. In 88 model-group scores, the number of watching tests passed had a weak association with echo agreement (rank correlation about +0.09). Using the pre-specified continuous rest score instead, the correlation with poke-pattern distance was −0.06 (p = 0.56). The 88 scores include repeated results from 30 model variants across held-out groups, so this does not prove that rest and poke performance are unrelated in general. It shows that passing these watching tests does not reliably identify the models that answer pokes correctly. Averaging each model over the held-out groups first, so that each of the 30 models counts once, gives −0.12 (p = 0.54).

Every kind of network that skipped pokes fails, whatever it is.

Is the failure about the linear model, or about something deeper? We put every family of artificial network we could build through the same test, on six held-out groups of worms: networks fitted to recordings; the same network with its connections dealt out at random; the classic model built from the wiring diagram with textbook physiology and fitted to nothing; networks trained to do a worm's job, steering towards food and away from harm, with or without the real wiring as a constraint and never shown a neural recording; and a published model fitted to this very atlas, run with its own stimulation model.

Only networks that learned from pokes answer a poke like a worm. Everything else falls four to a hundred times short, in every group. Among networks that never saw a poke, structure helps a little: the wiring diagram gets the most, a network that watched gets less, and the same network with random wiring gets nothing. The tests of ongoing activity do not put these networks in order: no family passes all six in any group, and the randomly wired network passes nearly as many as the one fitted to real worms.

Why: watching cannot see past shared signals it does not record.

A real worm cannot tell us its true cause and effect, so a model brain stands in: a published model of 154 neurons in which ongoing activity and responses to pokes come from the same dynamics. We record it the way the atlas was recorded, with half the neurons seen per recording, some never seen, and measurement noise, and try to learn its cause and effect from the recordings.

Watching works in principle. When each neuron's fluctuations are its own, it recovers cause and effect well, better than poking at the atlas's size. But add a few slow, shared, brain-wide signals that no recording captures, at a level that matches real resting worms, and watching hits a ceiling within a few hours that more data never lifts. Poking is not fooled by those signals: it keeps improving and overtakes watching between 40 and 70 hours of recording, the size of the atlas. A second published model as ground truth gives the same picture, and removing the strongest shared patterns before learning does not reliably help.

The real worms behave as predicted. A copy that learns by watching is no better after watching 58 worms than after 6 (echo agreement 0.004 to 0.014 throughout), while a copy given the pokes of just 6 worms already beats it four to seven times over, and keeps improving with more worms.

What this would mean. If real brains are driven by strong shared signals that recordings miss, a brain cannot be copied by watching it, however long. Intervention is then not a refinement but a necessity. These stand-in brains are linear and were themselves fitted to this atlas; the nonlinear test that follows confirms the ceiling and refines the explanation.

Where the truth is known, watching comes close to poking, and the test can be checked.

Real worms cannot tell us their true cause and effect, so we built artificial brains that can: nonlinear networks of 120 units, tuned so that two individuals' single-poke echoes agree about as well as two worms' do, with cause and effect known by construction. Recordings copy the atlas's design.

Watching versus poking. When every unit is recorded and nothing hidden drives them, a neural network that watches recovers cause and effect well (0.82), though poking still passes it with enough recording (0.91). Add slow hidden signals and none of these watching copies passes 0.62, however it is poked or however much memory it has, while poking reaches 0.82. Recorded like the atlas, half the units at a time, the watching copies stay at about 0.5 or below and poking reaches 0.65 to 0.88 by 40 hours. Poking passes every one of these watching copies by 40 hours of recording in every setting. (These copies all fill unrecorded units with zeros; a better-built copy, below, does much better.)

Taking the hidden signals out. Given the strongest slow patterns of its own recordings, a watching copy recovers most of what the hidden signals cost, from 0.61 to 0.77, when every unit is recorded. Recorded like the atlas, the same step changed nothing (0.30 to 0.30), and even the true hidden signals lifted it only to 0.48 against 0.76 for poking. We read this as a limit of the data. It was a limit of the copy.

Much of that was the copy. Every watching copy above fills the units a recording missed with zeros, so it learns from only part of its inputs. A copy that learns each pair of units only from the recordings where both were seen, with each unit's slow drift removed, changes the picture. Recorded like the atlas with slow hidden signals, it reaches 0.79 (from 0.34), against 0.85 for poking with the same drift correction; with less data than that it can lead (10 hours of each: 0.71 against 0.59), and with signals as fast as the responses it trails further (0.59 against 0.76). Built better, watching comes close to poking in artificial brains, but does not overtake it when poking has comparable data.

Scored exactly as worms are, the difference stays. Artificial individuals recorded like atlas worms (113 of them, about 2 minutes unpoked, then a poke every 30 seconds) and scored with the unchanged test: the better watching copy reaches 62% to 82% of how well individuals agree, beside 75% to 89% for the poke-trained copy, and passes in two of three groups when the hidden signals are strong. On real worms, built and scored the same way, watching copies reach 4% to 22% and the poke-trained copy 76% to 97%. Real worms are far harder to copy by watching than these artificial brains, and not because of the recording design, the amount of unpoked data or the score.

Looking for why (exploratory). We built candidate explanations into the artificial brains one at a time and scored each the same way. A slow calcium indicator changed nothing. Making their resting activity as smooth and low-dimensional as a worm's, whether through hidden outside signals or the network's own global modes, left them easy to copy by watching (84% to 107% of individuals' agreement). Only one change brought watching near the worms' level: connections that stay silent in ongoing activity but carry most of the response to a poke (watching 19% to 28%, poke-trained 82% to 86%). That shows one way the worm result can arise, not that worms work this way; anything a poke evokes that ongoing activity does not express would do the same, including stimulation side effects. Telling these apart needs the same neuron stimulated at several strengths.

The test, checked against the truth. A species of 60 artificial individuals, each recorded once like an atlas worm, split three ways as the atlas is, and scored with the unchanged test. Copies of known quality: the species-average brain, another individual, and the species brain with 10%, 25% or all of its connections moved; plus the two copies we built for worms. Where echoes are as reliable as worms', the test separates good copies from bad ones as the truth does, rejects every copy with a quarter of its connections moved, and certifies copies within about 20% of an individual. The watching copy reaches 48% to 70% of an individual's closeness and fails; the poke-trained copy reaches 80% to 90% and mostly passes, as on real worms.

The check also found limits. The test cannot rank close copies correctly (it put the poke-trained copy above a genuine individual where the truth puts it below). When individuals differ a lot, or hidden signals dominate, data the size of the atlas cannot certify anything. And with twice the individuals, the agreement rule fixed on 1 November becomes lenient enough to certify the watching copy in two of three groups, so it must be tightened before any larger dataset is scored.

No single ingredient makes an artificial network look like a worm.

Each explanation above was tested on its own. On 6 November we added five biological ingredients to the artificial brains in every combination (32 variants, two independently drawn brains each): slow background signals no recording sees, links that stay silent at rest, stimulation that spreads to neighbours, stimulation that tires with repeats, and modular wiring. Every variant, and the 113 worms, was measured on six properties by the same code: how much watching recovers, how wide echoes are, how much a repeated poke tires, how structured resting activity is, how individual each brain is, and how reproducible echoes are between individuals. The predictions were written down first.

The ingredients pull against each other. The slow background signals do most to degrade what watching recovers, and they give resting activity the worm's structure. Silent links make individuals' echoes as reproducible as worms'. No combination of the five on or off matches the worm on more than three of the six properties. We then searched over their strengths, round after round the same night. The closest networks combine strong background signals, some silent links, many units that stay quiet unless strongly driven, individuals that differ far more than in our default networks, and longer pokes. On the full test, watching recovers 11% to 29% of the agreement between their individuals (worms: 4% to 16%), and they come within about a fifth of the worm on average over five properties, though not within a quarter on every one. One worm property no network reproduces: stimulation that tires.

What we got wrong, and what independent reviews caught. We had predicted that background signals would matter most for watching; on the measure we pre-registered, silent links mattered most, but that measure is a ratio, and silent links lower it mainly by making individuals agree more. A later idea, that worm pokes stand out from the background more than ours, came from a broken measure; corrected, they do not. A claim that the networks lacked individuality only because of how hard we poked them did not hold either: what they lacked was individuals as different from each other as worms are. All of these corrections are in the research record.

Worm neurons answer less when asked twice. In 100 worms, a neuron stimulated a second time responds about a quarter less than slow drift explains (corrected for the fact that neurons poked again had larger first responses). Artificial networks answer the same way every time. How much the spread to other neurons falls is lost in noise.

The neuron, the tool, or both. Written down before we looked: if use tires a neuron, then a neuron recently driven by another neuron's poke, with no light on it, should answer its own poke less. Across neurons it answered more, which our first reading took as evidence for the tool. An independent review showed that comparison was confounded by how responsive each neuron is. Compared with itself, a neuron does answer less after being driven by others (exploratory), about a sixth as much per unit of drive as after being lit. In 74 people stimulated electrically, the wider network's responses do not tire across ten pulses five seconds apart, but that measures the network, not the stimulated tissue. So tiring stays a candidate difference, with its cause open. (Corrected 6 Nov; the earlier version said the drop belonged to the tool.)

Withdrawn 7 November. A control that moves every poke to a moment with no stimulation gave nearly the same values in worms and networks: the paragraph below was measuring the slow pace of ongoing activity, not the echo. Against clean quiet moments, pokes lengthen responses in worms and networks alike. Kept for the record.

An echo no network matches. After a poke, a worm's responses last about twice as long as an artificial network's (a mean of 6.5 frames above threshold, against 2.2 to 3.3), even when the networks are read through the same slow calcium signal and averaged the way worm neuron classes are. Drives, silent links and some quiet units bring echoes close to the worm's narrowness, and units that settle over a few seconds make them last longer (4.7 to 5.0, against 6.5). But those slow units spread echoes twice as wide, make individuals answer twice as alike and halve what watching recovers, and slowing only some units does not escape the trade-off. (Corrected twice after review: an earlier version said the lasting responses were the calcium indicator, and another overstated the slow units through a confound in how those networks were built.)

State in other brains. Mouse touch cortex leans the same way as worms (6 of 7 sessions, not significant). In 74 people, written down beforehand, the pattern a pulse produced did not change detectably with the cortex's state just before it, measured as the main pattern of its ongoing power. So the worm's kind of state dependence is not yet established beyond the worm.

The worm's background state changes where a poke goes. Judged by each worm's main pattern of resting activity, the same poke spreads differently in different states. A reviewer found this in the atlas worms; we then wrote down a replication test before looking and ran it on 15 other worms, never used for this test: it replicated, with a larger effect. A network built so that its slow drives change how its units respond shows the effect too, more weakly, and so does one of two versions of our closest network; simpler networks do not. Judged by AVA, a neuron that marks reversing, no change was detected, so the worm's main resting pattern is not simply its reversal state.

Not detected. Sensory neurons were not harder to copy by watching than interneurons or motor neurons, as we had predicted; the shares by role are too noisy to rank.

A stronger watcher fails the same way. A nonlinear watching copy, a recurrent network trained only on the worms' unstimulated stretches that learns their calm and active states, agrees with real worms' echoes at 0.004 to 0.009, against 0.09 to 0.11 between worms. On an artificial network where watching works, the same watcher recovers about half of the agreement between individuals, so it can succeed; on worms it does not.

A test that could not decide. If stimulation really activates a target's neighbours, their own synaptic partners should respond too. Our test of this found nothing, but its own positive control (the target's partners, which certainly respond) shows it could not have seen the effect at the expected size. Whether the neighbour effect is activation or light leaking into neighbouring recordings remains open.

On the data we have, nothing transfers across contexts.

Four more tests use every dataset we have: worms with neuropeptide release much reduced, freely moving worms, and worms meeting a copper boundary.

No sign that the hidden signals are neuropeptides. They spread through the body without the wiring, so they were the obvious suspect, and we predicted watching would do better in worms that release far fewer of them. It did not: watching stays near zero (0.009 against 0.017 in normal worms, in the same small design), and the shared slow structure is just as strong, about seven shared patterns with a timescale near 90 seconds in both strains. Watching sits at its floor in both, so a small gain could hide there. A synthetic worm given slow drives of this shape still shows the ceiling, but when those drives are weak, poking needs more than the atlas's 70 hours to overtake watching.

How many pokes a lab needs (exploratory). One worm's echo agrees with another's at only 0.07, and averaging helps more slowly than textbook noise would. Projecting from the largest groups we can measure, pinning one neuron's echo down takes at least about 160 worms poking it, against a median of 22 in the atlas. The feeding organ's own small nervous system gives echoes three times as reproducible as the rest; beyond it there is no clean rule.

Copies learned from freely moving worms fail the poke test. Fitted by watching 27 to 57 moving worms, they predict the next moment of activity well and answer a poke at about chance, like every other watching copy.

Meeting copper is not like poking its sensor. In moving worms, the brain-wide response to the copper boundary does not resemble the echo of poking ASH, the copper sensor, in still worms (r = −0.01), and no poked neuron's echo matches it better than chance would. This is one stimulus, and copper reaches other sensors too, so it is a warning rather than a law.

What this would mean. A faithful copy needs pokes, the brain state they were given in, and the context the copy will be used in. We know of no public dataset with all three for the same worms.

The failure is about cause and effect, not noise.

A fair objection: maybe a model knows the right causal structure, and each single echo is just buried in the wrong kind of variability. If so, averaging many simulated repeats of the experiment should uncover it. It does not. Even ten full repeats averaged give an echo that agrees with the atlas average less than one real worm does. The published model fitted to this atlas comes closest, and still falls short.

Worms seem to have causal fingerprints that watching does not reveal, though part may be off-target stimulation.

Downgraded, 8 Nov 2026. In artificial lab worms, stimulation that spills onto neighbouring neurons manufactures individual fingerprints like these, and they vanish without the spillover. In the atlas, pokes whose targets share neighbours do give slightly more similar echoes (+0.019, permutation p = 0.002, though the worm-level interval includes zero). A test matched for neighbours could not be calibrated at this data size, so it was not read. The fingerprint below is therefore possibly partly off-target stimulation, not established individuality.

If real worms all answered pokes the same way, the failures above would just mean the models are imprecise. They do not. Split each worm's pokes into two halves: the two halves of the same worm agree with each other better than with other worms, in 36 of 43 worms (p = 5 × 10−6). The effect is not a recording-day artefact; worms recorded on the same day are no closer than worms from other days.

So we asked the sharper question. Fit a model to one worm's own resting activity. Does it predict that same worm's echoes better than models of other worms do? No: own model closer in 26 of 44 worms, median difference −0.008, p = 0.11. Each brain has its own wiring of cause and effect, and watching that brain at rest does not tell you what it is.

On the repaired data, with responses in units of each neuron's fluctuations, a worm's two halves agree at 0.17 and different worms at 0.09, across 94 worms, and the gap holds when the halves are the first and second half of the recording. Two explanations remain open: genuine individuality, or worm-specific measurement such as neuron identities assigned once per worm. Can one particular worm then be copied from its own recording? Barely: predicting a worm's second half from a blend of its own first half and the average worm beats the average worm alone by 0.007. In the atlas a pulse mostly hits a new neuron, about 1.8 pulses per stimulated class per worm, so copying one animal needs repeated pokes of the same neurons in the same worm. That is the experiment we have asked for.

What it took to answer a poke like a worm.

Nonlinear neurons, sparse wiring and training directly on pokes each failed on their own. The first brain to pass the poke test, with its echo pattern and spread inside in all three held-out groups (and, on the repaired data, shown to agree on the echo pattern within a tolerance fixed in advance in all six), needed four things together: causal links learned from pokes as links rather than as one-step dynamics; ongoing dynamics learned by watching; fast noise that does not propagate; and a brain that alternates between calm and active stretches.

The pass replicates: on a second, independently drawn split of the same worms, its echo pattern and spread are again inside in all three held-out groups. It is not the end of the story. That brain is weaker at rest than the plain linear model (three or four watching tests of six, against five), and its echoes are still too complex. Each fix we tried since moved one property toward real worms and pushed another away.

One model passes both halves of the test, but only on the worms it was built on.

The brain above passed the poke test but was weaker at rest. Searching for one model that passes both, round by round, each step guided by where the last one failed, led to a recipe of four ingredients: slow brain-wide states that interact with each other; each worm its own dynamics; causal links learned from pokes; and neuron-level observation, which is about the measurement rather than the biology. Coupling between neuron classes in the fast dynamics turned out not to be needed.

On the atlas worms the recipe passes the rule set in advance (at least five of six activity tests and all three poke tests) in every one of ten noise draws, on two different splits of the worms (Figure 8b). Some tests pass narrowly: echo size and spread pass because their excess is within worm-to-worm variation, not because it is absent. Ablations on an earlier version, exploratory, point to what matters: without causal links from pokes the echo pattern fails in every group; without the slow states, correlations become too typical and echo size fails.

But the recipe was built on those worms, and no unseen wild-type stimulated worms remain to confirm it. Refitted from scratch on the atlas's unc-31 mutant worms, which played no part in building it, it passes in three of five draws, one short of the four set in advance. On another lab's freely moving worms it passes one of six activity tests in every draw, worse than a plain model fitted to rest. What a copy needs seems to depend on whether the worm is moving, or on the atlas lab's conditions; this data cannot separate the two.

Given what it lacked, does it hold? An exploratory look had named two things the recipe was missing on moving worms: coupling between neuron classes, which it had dropped, and the lopsided shape of real activity. A registered study gave it both and tested it on 27 freely moving worms that no version of the recipe had met, with the rule frozen first and a single run allowed. The failure repeated: as built, the recipe passes 1 to 3 of 6. With both changes it passes 4 of 6 in every one of five draws, better on the tests the new ingredient does not pass by construction, and one short of the pass mark of five. Coupling does most of the work. With that change alone the recipe reached 5 of 6 in two of three draws, though that version was not a registered contender and no unseen moving worms remain to test it on. The shape ingredient fixes the distribution of activity but slows the simulated worm, which costs it the timescale and reversal-timing tests. The review before the freeze also found that the simulator drew part of its noise in an order that changed from run to run, so a seed did not fix a result; that is corrected, and earlier counts for this recipe were each one draw from that spread.

In a real brain, most of the brain stays out of the echo.

Suspended 7 November. The per-poke complexity maps give nearly the same values at moments with no poke (worms 0.66 with a poke, 0.68 without), so this section describes ongoing activity, not a property of the echo. Results built on responses averaged over many pokes are unaffected.

Consciousness research judges unresponsive patients by the complexity of the brain's echo to a magnetic pulse: the perturbational complexity index, which separates wakefulness from deep sleep, anaesthesia and coma. The optogenetic atlas is the same experiment in a worm, thousands of times over. Applied there, it is the one property no artificial brain matches yet.

We expected models to fail by being too simple. They fail the other way: their echoes are more complex than real ones. After a poke, a third of a real worm's neuron classes never respond within 30 seconds, and the ones that do keep responding. In every model, almost every class flickers across the threshold at some point, briefly. The restlessness comes from the models' ongoing dynamics rather than their causal links, which is why it survived every fix to the links.

Not a claim about consciousness. Nothing here says a worm or a model is conscious. These are the measurable properties that, in people, separate a responsive brain from an unresponsive one.

At rest, the same brains pass the consciousness-science measures.

Consciousness science has three standard measures of ongoing activity: how complex it is, how integrated its parts are, and how rich its repertoire of whole-brain states is. We expected linear models to come out too irregular. They do not: per-worm linear brains match real worms on all three, on two independent datasets. Measures of ongoing activity, however principled, cannot tell these copies from the real thing. The echo can.

A worm neuron poked again soon answers about half as strongly.

Take every time the atlas poked the same neuron twice, compare the second response with the previous one, and subtract the same comparison for two different neurons poked the same time apart. 0.6 to 2 minutes later a neuron answers at about half its previous response; after 2 to 5 minutes about two thirds; after 5 to 10 minutes it is not detectably weaker. About 9% of the atlas's pokes are repeats within 5 minutes. We found no report of this in the atlas paper, its tool paper or the models fitted to it.

Is it the neuron or the light-activated tool? We tried two ways of separating them, written down first, and checked both on synthetic worms where we knew the answer. Both failed, so neither was used on the real worms: every direct poke delivers light and activity together. Two things do come through. A neuron driven by other neurons, with no light on it, answers a little less next time (about 10% per unit of drive), beyond what synthetic worms without neuron fatigue produce; and the decline persists in mutants with reduced neuropeptide release. So probably both contribute. (An independent review caught that our first version included repeats only 30 seconds apart, whose baseline still held the previous response; it said 40% instead of about half.)

The same question in a mouse. The mouse recordings differ in one useful way: the driven cells' own spikes are recorded, so their answer to the light can be read directly. In 39 mice, cells carrying a light-gated channel were driven 300 times, two seconds apart, for ten minutes. A registered study, reviewed before its rule was frozen and run once, compared the last third of the block with the first. The driven cells give 98% as many spikes to a brief pulse at the end as at the start (95 to 99.5%): a small, reliable fade, nowhere near the worm's halving, and above the worm's range in 39 of 39 mice. That measure cannot see the first flashes. An independent check after the result looked at them: in the 24 mice whose block opens with a pulse, the very first answer is about a fifth larger than later ones, and the drop is over by the third pulse. That look was not registered and rests on one trial per mouse.

The study also asked whether the answer of the surrounding cortex fades faster than the driven cells' own. It does, by about a tenth, in both registered versions of the measure, with resting rates unchanged. But a control added after the result takes the edge off: in 27 mice with no channel, the response to the light alone, smaller and far less repeatable, also tends to shrink over the block, by an amount these data cannot tell apart from that tenth. So the tenth cannot be pinned on anything tiring with use; the cortex may simply answer less as ten minutes pass. None of this says why the worm's answer halves. It is a different animal, a different light tool, a different spacing and a different readout, and in the mouse too the early drop could belong to the channel or to the cell.

In the other direction: brains change their rules, plain networks do not.

Every candidate above is something worms do. Asked the other way round, what artificial networks do that brains do not, the first answer is that they keep the same rules. Compare how each unit's activity predicts the next moment early and late in a recording, against a control that mixes the halves. Networks without slow processes, rate or spiking, give the same answer in both halves. Worms drift (+0.011 with the strict rest rule, +0.059 with a looser one), and so does human cortex (+0.040). Slow hidden signals or fatigue make a network drift too (+0.021), but less than worms: a difference of degree, not kind.

Spiking networks behave like the others.

Plain spiking networks can be copied by watching and do not tire, like the rate networks, and as built they are less worm-like in echo breadth, resting structure and individuality. Every fatigue we built into the spiking neurons themselves made the second poke look larger, not smaller, on the worm's measure; only a stimulation input that weakens with use gave the worm's sign.

Part two

Beyond the worm: mouse cortex and seven kinds of network

Recorded through each mouse's own instrument, a random network has less slow memory than the mouse.

The worm results raise a question they cannot answer alone: do the same differences appear in a larger brain, and with other kinds of network? We took the Allen Brain Observatory's Neuropixels recordings of mouse visual cortex (58 mice) and asked first how much a network's measurements depend on how it is recorded. A recording is a brain seen through an instrument: its bin width, its spikes per bin, its share of unstructured variance, its spread of firing rates. We measured those numbers in each mouse, built a simulated copy of each mouse's instrument, and recorded artificial networks through it.

The instrument mattered more than the animals did. A plain 480-unit random network, recorded through a median instrument, had a slow memory (0.107) inside the mice's range (0.031 to 0.228), so the registered test that networks have less slow memory was refuted as worded. But recording the same network through five single mice's instruments moved its value by 1.8 times the spread between mice, and by the registered transfer rule the comparison was not testable at all.

Compared mouse by mouse, the picture is clear (Figure 12). In spontaneous activity, the random network has less slow memory than the mouse in 33 of 35 mice, by a median 1.4 between-mouse SDs (90% interval 1.24 to 1.59). With strong slow drives added, it has more than all 31 mice measured, so cortex sits between the two. Tuned on 17 mice, a drive carrying about 6% of each unit's variance brought the network to the typical mouse on 18 held-out mice (median difference +0.03 SDs), but the interval (−0.08 to +0.73) was too wide to show equivalence, and the network did not reproduce how mice differ from each other.

Cortex does not carry more, slower internal state than artificial networks.

That result fitted our earlier reading, that brains carry state their activity does not show. So we tested its broad form directly, pre-registered, with three independent reviews before the data and one after: does mouse visual cortex carry more internal state than artificial networks (activity not locked to the input), and is that state slower relative to the input? The stimulus was a 30-second natural film shown 20 or 60 times. Both measures compare repeats within one system, so no clock has to be matched across systems. In the mice, a median 59% of state is internal, and it is faster than the input-locked part in 48 of 49 mice (median ratio 0.69). Both measures were reliable across mice (split-half rank correlations 0.96 and 0.83), and a shuffled-film control behaved as it should.

Seven families of network were recorded through each mouse's own instrument: a random recurrent network at eight input gains, a task-trained recurrent network, GRU and closed-form continuous-time (CfC) networks, a spiking network, Pythia transformers from 14 million to 1 billion parameters, and Mamba state-space models. None of the four claims held (Figure 13). The networks span the mice: GRU and CfC carry far less internal state, the task-trained network and Mamba more, and the range of transformer sizes contains the median mouse. Larger transformers came closer than the smallest, but not in order of size. On relative slowness, no counted family was shown to be faster than the mice; GRU was shown slower, and for CfC, Pythia and Mamba the difference points the same way, within the known measurement bias.

Three caveats travel with this. Nine mice without a measurable film timescale were left out after their values were seen, and they differ from the rest. The spiking network could not be matched to each mouse's instrument. And whether the mice's internal state counts as slow depends on the input: with the film's frames shuffled, the locked part collapses and the ratio rises to about 33.

In mouse cortex, watching cannot tell which way a cell pushes the brain.

The worm result says a copy that only watched answers a poke wrongly. We asked the same of mouse cortex, using the Allen Brain Observatory's optotagging sessions: mice in which one kind of inhibitory cell (Sst, Vip or Pvalb) carries a light-sensitive channel, recorded with Neuropixels probes while a one-second pulse of light switched those cells on. For each mouse we built two copies from the classes of cells recorded (brain area by waveform): one fitted to resting activity only, one fitted to the mouse's other light conditions. Neither ever saw the test condition.

The first test missed its bar. Its primary claim, that the stimulation-fitted copy beats the watching copy by a large margin mouse by mouse, was written down first, checked on synthetic networks recorded through three real mice's instruments, reviewed independently, and frozen before any response was read. The checks caught two flaws in our own method (a baseline overlapping the previous light, and light artefacts that tagged cells in mice without the channel), and both were fixed before any data. On the 9 mice that entered (8 of them reliable enough to score), the median gap cleared its bar (0.47 against 0.4) but its lower bound did not (−0.08 against 0.3), so the claim is reported as not supported.

What it showed instead. The copy fitted at rest predicted that switching on the inhibitory cells would raise activity in most regions. In all 8 Pvalb and Sst mice most regions fell; in the one Vip mouse, whose cells mainly inhibit other inhibitory cells, most rose. That was found after looking, so it needed new mice. A retest on the 15 unused mice of the same dataset could not decide: 14 of them had no light-responsive cells.

The registered retest (Figure 14). In a second dataset, Allen Visual Behavior Neuropixels, we wrote five claims down and froze them before computing any response, then ran them once on the 39 mice with enough light-responsive cells. All five held. Switching on Sst cells lowered most regions in 29 of 29 mice (a median 13% rose); the copy fitted at rest predicted the wrong direction in 28 of 29 (it expected 77% to rise); Sst cells rose and fell with the rest of the brain at rest in 28 of 29; switching on Vip cells raised most regions in 10 of 10; and the copy's error was larger for Sst than for Vip mice. The first two hold with visual cortex only and with broad-waveform cells only.

Two checks narrow what this means. An independent check found no error and confirmed the result under other timing choices, but showed that the copy fitted at rest predicts a rise for almost any group of cells: chosen at random, visual-cortex units give the same prediction. So the failure is not peculiar to Sst cells; rather, resting co-activity carries no information about the sign of a cell's effect. And the light alone raises about two thirds of regions in mice without the channel, so the Vip effect beyond the light is real but small; the Sst effect is far beyond it. One session per mouse, one rig, two cell types, and the tests are about direction: on the detailed pattern, a copy fitted to light pulses does better than the watcher (median agreement 0.57 against −0.06 in Sst mice).

Does a copy taught by pulses answer like a second mouse? A third registered study asked this of the same 39 mice, with the rule frozen before any agreement between two mice was computed. The yardstick is the project’s own: how well one mouse’s response to the slow light predicts another’s. In 29 Sst mice the copy taught by brief pulses predicts that most regions fall, on the right side in 27 of 29, and its pattern across regions agrees with the mouse at 0.60 where a second mouse reaches 0.36 (above the second mouse in 26 of 29). It is also within a quarter of what the average of the other mice achieves. By the rule set in advance it answers like a second mouse, and the copy fitted at rest does not. Two things from the independent check limit what that means. First, the copy explains nothing: in this protocol its prediction is each region’s own pulse response replayed, and the raw pulse response does as well with no fitting (0.63). Second, in mice with no light-sensitive cells a mouse’s own pulse response still predicts its slow-light pattern better than a second mouse does (0.33 against 0.12), so part of the pattern match is what any predictor taken from the same mouse gets for free; the direction result has no such counterpart. Mice agree with each other only at 0.36, and the copy is far below what the recording’s own reliability allows (about 0.97). A review before the freeze also found that units silent at rest could wreck a region’s prediction; this study leaves them out.

Beyond the worm: in mouse hippocampus, the test is not yet sharp enough.

The same question in mice: hundreds of cells imaged in hippocampal area CA1 for about 20 untouched minutes, then one inhibitory neuron stimulated about ten times with light (open data from the Cossart lab). Each session is its own individual. The prediction, analysis and expectations were written down before any trace was opened; amendments made later are listed with the results.

In the 16 sessions that passed every check, ranking cells by how they moved together with the stimulated neuron while it was only being watched ordered their responses to stimulation weakly (rank correlation about 0.15), where distance from the stimulated neuron ordered nothing. A dynamical copy added nothing over that simple correlation, and accounting for running changed nothing. Two of our three predictions were not borne out as written.

But a control undercuts it. Run at fake stimulation times, when nothing was poked, half as many sessions pass the checks and watching "predicts" those non-responses too, only somewhat less well; the difference is not significant. So this data does not show that watching predicts responses to pokes in mouse CA1, as opposed to the ordinary co-activity our inclusion check selects for. More than half the sessions show no clear response in the stimulated neuron at all, and a PC crash cut the download short; a recovery pass will add the remaining sessions.

Part three

Taken together

What it adds up to.

No property we measured separates brains from artificial networks in kind. In the worm, each difference either dissolved when a network was given the right ingredient, or turned out to measure ongoing activity rather than the brain's answer to a poke. In mouse cortex, the broad form of our earlier reading, that brains carry more and slower internal state, failed a direct, pre-registered test: the networks span the mice.

What survives is narrower, and practical. A copy of a brain cannot be judged by watching it; it has to be stimulated, and it has to be recorded the way the animal was. Judged that way, every gap we could close closed when the network was given the right data, drive or training. So far the difference looks like information, what a network was shown and built to carry, rather than material. In mouse cortex the same lesson holds in its plainest form: a copy fitted at rest cannot even tell which way switching on a cell pushes the brain. That is a reading across these results, not a law, and three gaps remain open: whether a neuron or the light tool tires on a repeated poke, why a copy built on still worms fails on moving ones, and whether a copy taught by stimulation can answer like a second mouse. Each needs data that is not public.

Where the data runs out.

Everything that can be done with existing data has now been tried at least once. The last test asked whether recording more of one worm helps it be copied by watching. The atlas names a median of 72 neurons per worm but tracks about 123, and the unnamed ones are recorded, only not identifiable across worms. Within single worms, a watching copy that includes them does slightly better than one with named neurons only (better in 26 of 44 worms), and taking out slow patterns adds nothing. But each worm offers only about 3 usable minutes of unpoked activity, and the average of other worms' pokes predicts each worm's own echoes far better (0.22 against 0.05) in 43 of 44 worms.

What would answer the rest, in order of what it would unlock:

  1. Whole-brain recordings with nearly every neuron identified, 30 to 60 minutes long, in worms that are then poked. If missing neurons are why watching fails in worms, a well-built watching copy should then come close to predicting that worm's own pokes. If it does not, worms differ from our artificial brains in a deeper way.
  2. Repeated pokes of the same neurons in the same worm, to build and check a copy of one individual.
  3. Many more pokes per neuron, about 160 worms each for a reliable echo, aimed where echoes are least reliable.
  4. Pokes in freely moving worms, with the worm's behaviour recorded.
  5. Reporters for whatever carries the slow shared signals.

Two checks on the headline.

It is about which neuron was poked. If most of what follows a poke were the same whichever neuron was poked, such as a startle or a side effect of the light, poke-trained copies could pass just by learning that one response. A copy that knows only that something was poked, not what, fails as badly as the watching copies (echo agreement 0.001 to 0.012, against 0.08 to 0.09 for the poke-trained copy and about 0.1 between real worms). The test measures which neuron causes what.

It survives independent code. A standalone script using none of our pipeline, with its own simple definitions, gives the same order: watching does a fifth to a quarter as well as the average of other worms' pokes. How far watching falls short of a real worm depends on how noise is handled, so we quote that number with care.

What this does not show.

  • It does not show that any model, or any worm, is conscious.
  • Neuron classes, not single neurons, are the unit throughout; left and right partners are averaged.
  • Calcium imaging is slow and indirect. A brain that matches these recordings matches what the microscope can see.
  • The brain that passes the poke test was confirmed on a second, independently drawn split of the worms (echo pattern and spread inside in all three new groups). Both splits come from one lab's recordings.
  • The test has a measured resolution: it never flags real worms, always catches errors of half, and often misses errors of about a quarter. A pass means within about 25% of a real worm on these tests.
  • On 1 November three of the 113 recordings were found to have been cut short when the archive was unpacked, and were rebuilt. Results marked as rerun use the repaired data; older figures are labelled.
  • Why real worms resist watching is not known. Among the mechanisms we built into artificial brains, only pathways that are silent at rest reproduced it; that is a constructed demonstration of one possibility, not evidence about worms.
  • Nothing learned in still, immobilised worms has been shown to carry over to freely moving ones; the emulator built on still worms fails on them, and comes one test short once given what it lacked.
  • In mouse, one region (visual cortex), one film per test, and only state within a 30-second repeat. Networks other than the spiking one are seen through a spike model, which turns additive changes into multiplicative ones.
  • No brain passes everything; each fix so far has cost something elsewhere.
