A ruler for brains, measured in real worms.

There is no single right answer for what a worm's brain should do; healthy worms differ. So NEMATICA never asks whether a brain matches a worm. It asks whether the brain differs from real worms by about as much as real worms differ from each other.

Inside, outside, or too typical.

For every test, we measure the distance between every pair of real worms in a held-out group. That spread of distances is the ruler. An artificial brain is run through the same experiment as each real worm (same length, same stimulation schedule) and the same distance is measured from its simulated worm to every real worm except the one it imitates.

The verdict comes from resampling whole worms, so that one strange worm cannot decide it. A brain is outside when its distances are reliably larger than real worms' distances from each other, or its median lies beyond the 95th percentile. It is too typical when its distances are reliably smaller, or below the 5th percentile: it is closer to every worm than worms are to each other, which is what an average worm looks like. Otherwise it is inside.

Why too typical is a failure. A model trained to predict well converges on the average worm. No real worm is average, and a copy of a particular brain should not be either. In the first arc of this project, the model tuned to forecast best was too typical on several tests, while a slightly worse forecaster sat on the real-worm line.

Before scoring any model, we break real brains on purpose.

A test is only trusted once it gives the answers we wrote down in advance for systems whose truth we know. We take real worms and damage them in specific ways: read their responses at random times instead of after the poke, credit the wrong neuron with each poke, triple every response, or play responses backwards. Each damage should fail some tests and pass others. The poke tests hit 31 of 33 designed outcomes; the consciousness-science tests hit 24 of 27.

How sharp the poke test is.

Real worms agree only weakly on which neuron answers which poke, so it is fair to ask how much true cause and effect a brain needs before the test notices. We built synthetic brains whose echoes are a known blend of the true average echo and the same echoes reassigned to random pairs. The blend fixes exactly how strongly the brain's echoes correlate with the truth, and every echo keeps its real size and shape.

The test turns out to be narrow and precise. Below a correlation of about 0.3 with the true average echo, a brain is outside: it does not know enough cause and effect. Above about 0.5, it is too typical: more stereotyped than any worm. The window is 0.15 to 0.2 wide, and it sits in the same place in all three held-out groups. A brain copy has to be right about cause and effect and as individual as a real worm.

Rules that keep the benchmark honest.

  1. Predictions, including what would count as failure, are written into the project's record before each experiment runs. Misses are reported next to hits.
  2. Worms are split into groups once, by worm, frozen with a hash and never regenerated after results are seen.
  3. A brain is never compared with the worm it was derived from.
  4. Every change to the ruler is checked against calibration systems with known answers.
  5. Every quantity carries a tag: measured, inferred, learned, or assumed.
  6. Recordings normalised over the whole session leak the future into the past; only the original traces are used.
