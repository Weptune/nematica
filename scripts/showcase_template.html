NEMATICAAn open study of what separates real nervous systems from artificial ones

Looking like a brain is not the same as working like one.

We copied the brain of a worm, C. elegans, many different ways and tested every copy twice: does it look real while it rests, and does it answer like a real worm when one neuron is switched on? Try the test yourself below.

What the tests found

Every prediction was written down before the data was analysed; when one failed, the failure stayed in the record. Seven independent reviews checked the numbers, and two of their corrections changed what is shown here.

So what separates them?

  • Found

    A copy cannot be checked by watching it. Realism at rest and realism under pokes are unrelated across 30 models (rank correlation -0.12), and each worm's causal fingerprint is invisible in its resting activity.

  • Found

    Neurons that tire with use, and a background state that steers where a signal goes. Both are in worms and absent from every ordinary artificial network we tested, rate, spiking or transformer.

  • By degree

    Brains drift; plain networks keep their rules. Worms and human cortex change how activity predicts the next moment over a recording. Networks drift only when given slow hidden processes, and less.

  • Withdrawn

    An echo that is narrow and lasting at once. It looked like the worm's most distinctive trait until a control without any poke reproduced it: it was the slow pace of ongoing activity.

  • Our reading

    The difference is what a system carries outside its units' activity. Every difference traces to state that a recording does not show: slow signals no neuron carries, changes left behind by use, switches between calm and active stretches. Give a network that kind of hidden state and it acquires the worm's properties. This is a reading across the results, not yet a direct test.
