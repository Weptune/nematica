REAL AND ARTIFICIAL BRAINS, NEURON BY NEURON

We built artificial brains that pass for real.
Then we poked them.

NEMATICA records every neuron in a living worm's head and asks whether an artificial brain can be the same thing: not just look the same, but respond the same. We test it the way doctors test a brain that cannot speak: watch it at rest, then perturb it and read the echo.

WHAT WE ARE TRYING TO DO

Find out what a real brain has that a convincing artificial one does not.

People are building digital nervous systems, and one day someone will claim an artificial brain is a faithful copy of a real one. Nobody agrees how to check that claim. We are building the check, and running it on the only brain small enough to compare completely.

WHY A WORM
302 neurons, every connection mapped.
C. elegans is the only animal whose complete wiring diagram is known, and whose whole head can be imaged neuron by neuron while it lives. If an artificial brain cannot match a worm, it cannot match anything bigger.

The yardstick: real worms are all different.
No two real worm brains behave the same, so "close to a real brain" needs a unit. Ours is how different real worms are from each other. An artificial brain passes a test when it is as different from real worms as they are from one another. Further away is wrong. Closer is wrong too: it means the model learned an average worm that does not exist.

THE FINDING SO FAR

Looks real at rest. Wrong when poked.

In 113 real worms, researchers switched on one neuron at a time with light and recorded how the whole brain answered. We gave our artificial brains exactly the same pulses. The model below passes five of our six tests of ordinary brain activity on these very worms. Its echoes are still wrong: they go to the wrong neurons, and they spread much too far.

Each row is one type of neuron; each column is one second after a light pulse to the SMD neurons. Green cells rose, orange cells fell; brighter means a larger change. The same holds across all 113 worms and all three groups of held-out worms: the artificial brain's echoes agree with real worms at about 0.01 on our pattern score (a correlation), while two real worms agree with each other at about 0.10. Even the published models built for this very atlas reach only 0.03 to 0.08.

SIGNATURES OF A MIND

Measured with the tools of consciousness science.

How do doctors tell whether a patient who cannot move or speak is conscious? One of the best tests pulses the brain with a magnet and measures the complexity of the echo. Awake brains answer with echoes that are widespread and differentiated. Deep sleep and anaesthesia give a local blip or one big uniform wave.

The worm atlas is the same experiment, thousands of times over, so we measure the same thing in real and artificial worm brains.

We do not claim a worm, or a model, is conscious; nobody can measure experience directly. We ask a question that can be answered: do artificial brains have the measurable properties that, in people, separate a responsive brain from an unresponsive one?
